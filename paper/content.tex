\section{\textcolor{black}{Introduction}}
% Natural Language Processing (NLP) has made significant progress in recent years. It allows computers to analyze, interpret, and generate human language. Modern methods for understanding and generating natural language rely on models built on the Transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention,raffel2020exploring}. Large Language Models (LLMs) based on this structure have been used for various NLP tasks in fields like healthcare, finance, and legal systems \cite{dubey2024llama3,gemma2024}.

% NLP has encountered several challenges when it comes to low-resource languages, especially regional dialects of higher-resource languages \cite{joshi2020state,joshi2025survey}. Surveys among communities that speak these dialects show that understanding and analyzing dialectal text is a priority \cite{blaschke2024what}. This highlights the need to convert non-standard dialects into a standard form while preserving the original meaning \cite{kuparinen2023dialect}. Improved NLP methods now make it possible to build systems that connect previously inaccessible low-resource regional dialects.

% India recognizes 22 languages constitutionally, with hundreds of regional dialects spoken by over 1.4 billion people. While languages like Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, and Tamil have gained attention through initiatives like AI4Bharat IndicCorp and BPCC \cite{dabre2022indicbart}, sub-regional dialects remain largely neglected in NLP. An example of this linguistic challenge is Marathi, an Indo-Aryan language spoken by over 83 million people, mainly in Maharashtra \cite{dabre2022indicbart}. Marathi is written in Devanagari from left to right and is one of India's 22 constitutionally recognized languages. While Standard Marathi (which is based on the variety from Pune and nearby areas) has a clear literary standard and established digital infrastructure, regional sub-dialects often show differences in pronunciation and vocabulary compared to the standard form.
Recent advances in deep learning and self-attention mechanisms \cite{vaswani2017attention} have driven rapid progress across natural language understanding and sequence-to-sequence generation tasks.
The widespread adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{dubey2024llama3} and open-weight foundational architectures \cite{gemma2024} has enabled specialized text processing across domains such as finance, healthcare, and digital governance.
However, current language technology ecosystems remain predominantly tailored toward standardized, high-resource national languages, creating a substantial performance bottleneck when applied to non-standard regional dialects \cite{joshi2020state}.
Community surveys and global NLP evaluations \cite{blaschke2024what,joshi2025survey} emphasize that native dialect speakers experience severe system degradation in downstream tasks---such as Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Machine Translation (MT), and conversational interfaces---due to unstandardized orthography, phonetic variation, and vocabulary shifts \cite{koenecke2020racial,kuparinen2023dialect}.

Dialect normalization---the task of transducing non-standard, regional dialectal text into normative written language while strictly preserving underlying semantic intent \cite{kuparinen2023dialect}---serves as an essential pre-processing layer to ensure digital linguistic inclusion.
In multilingual environments such as India, which encompasses 22 scheduled languages alongside hundreds of unwritten regional dialects spoken by 1.4 billion people, sub-regional varieties remain drastically neglected.
Marathi, an Indo-Aryan scheduled language spoken by over 83 million people in western India \cite{dabre2022indicbart}, provides a salient case study.
While Standard Marathi (centered around the Pune literary register) possesses codified orthographic norms and digital infrastructure, regional sub-dialects---such as Southern Konkan (Malvani), Northern Konkan (Ahirani/Khandeshi), and Varhadi (Vidarbha)---exhibit marked phonological, lexical, and morpho-syntactic divergence from the normative written standard \cite{kulkarni2023variation,tarfe2024linguistic}.

Building computational normalization systems for regional Indic dialects presents two core challenges:
(1) \textit{Extreme Data Scarcity}: parallel corpora mapping unstandardized regional dialects to normative standard forms are virtually non-existent; and
(2) \textit{Agglutinative Morphological Complexity}: Marathi features rich inflectional morphology, where noun case markers, postpositional affixes, and verbal tense/aspect suffixes undergo complex subword transformations rather than simple surface dictionary lookup \cite{ansary2024unicode,partanen2019dialect}.

To address these limitations, this work establishes a multi-dialect Marathi text normalization benchmark utilizing synthetic corpus expansion and sequence-to-sequence transformer fine-tuning.
Our core contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Curated Parallel Dialect Corpus}: Grounded in the RESPIN read-speech transcripts \cite{kumar2025respin}, we construct and release a parallel corpus mapping three major Marathi sub-dialects (Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi) to Standard Marathi across agricultural and banking domains.
    \item \textbf{Automated Synthetic Augmentation with Multi-Tier Verification}: We design a domain-constrained synthetic generation engine using Gemma-4 \cite{gemma2024} coupled with a multi-tier verification pipeline (Devanagari script integrity filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B \cite{dubey2024llama3} semantic auditing) that purges corrupted pairs and expands the parallel corpus to 32,335 verified sentence pairs.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Validated Neural Benchmarks}: We conduct 5-fold cross-validated empirical evaluations comparing multilingual sequence-to-sequence architectures (\texttt{mT5-small} and IndicBART) across single-dialect, multi-dialect, original, and synthetically expanded dataset partitions, evaluating model scaling, verification engine ablation, and subword morphological error patterns.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Historical and User-Generated Text Normalization}}

Text normalization encompasses both historical spelling modernization and the transduction of User-Generated Content (UGC).
Early comparative evaluations by Pettersson et al. \cite{pettersson2014multilingual} and Scherrer and Erjavec \cite{scherrer2016normalising} established Character-level Statistical Machine Translation (CSMT) as an effective baseline for modernizing archaic European text.
Multilingual evaluations by Tang et al. \cite{tang2018evaluation} and Bollmann \cite{bollmann2019historical} across seven languages revealed that subword modeling via Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) consistently outperforms character-level models, while Bawden et al. \cite{bawden2022automatic} confirmed that sentence-level context resolves ambiguous historical spelling variants.

In digital social media text, De Clercq et al. \cite{declercq2013normalization} demonstrated that combining character- and word-level SMT architectures addresses informal slang and phonetic typos.
Matos Veliz et al. \cite{matosveliz2019data} showed that data augmentation is vital for enabling neural models to normalize noisy user input.
Frameworks such as MoNoise \cite{vandergoot2019monoise} modeled noisy text as edit operations, while the MultiLexNorm benchmark \cite{vandergoot2021multilexnorm} confirmed that lexical normalization directly enhances downstream parsing and POS tagging.
Samuel and Straka \cite{samuel2021ufal} further established the efficacy of fine-tuning byte-level architectures (byT5 \cite{xue2021mt5}) for word-level sequence transduction.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Global Dialect-to-Standard Text Normalization}}

Dialect-to-standard normalization transduces non-standard regional varieties into normative standard forms.
In European languages, Scherrer and Ljube{\v{s}}i{\'c} \cite{scherrer2016swiss} applied CSMT to Swiss German, demonstrating that sentence context resolves ambiguous dialectal word forms, while Lusetti et al. \cite{lusetti2018impact} showed neural encoder-decoder architectures surpass SMT when augmented with target language models.
Kresic and Abbas \cite{kresic2024normalizing} benchmarked \texttt{mT5-small} fine-tuning on the SwissDial corpus, showing performance competitive with larger models.
Partanen et al. \cite{partanen2019dialect} normalized 23 Finnish dialects using sliding-window LSTMs and character Transformers, while H{\"a}m{\"a}l{\"a}inen et al. \cite{hamalainen2020normalizing,hamalainen2022generating} explored Swedish and Estonian dialect normalization.
Kuparinen et al. \cite{kuparinen2023dialect} conducted a multi-lingual evaluation across four language families, proving that fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence transformers and byte-level models (byT5) consistently outperform statistical baselines.

In East Asian and Afroasiatic varieties, Abe et al. \cite{abe2018multi} translated Japanese regional dialects using multi-dialectal LSTMs operating on \emph{bunsetsu} phrases.
For Arabic dialects, CODA \cite{habash2012coda} codified conventional orthography for Modern Standard Arabic (MSA), extended by MADAR \cite{bouamor2018madar} across 25 Arab cities, Eryani et al. \cite{eryani2020arabic}, and NADI shared tasks \cite{abdulmageed2023nadi}.
Zhao and Chodroff \cite{zhao2022mandarin} analyzed Mandarin dialect variation, and Dimakis et al. \cite{dimakis2025dialect} combined morphological rules with LLM few-shot prompting for Greek dialect proverbs.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Indic NLP and Marathi Regional Dialects}}

Despite high linguistic heterogeneity, dialect normalization remains critically underexplored for Indic languages \cite{joshi2020state,joshi2025survey,zampieri2020nlp}.
Pre-trained sequence-to-sequence architectures like IndicBART \cite{dabre2022indicbart} leverage mBART-50 \cite{lewis2020bart} with unified Devanagari representation across 11 languages, while mT5 \cite{xue2021mt5} extends T5 \cite{raffel2020exploring} across 101 languages, making its subword vocabulary suitable for dialectal transformations.
Monolingual Marathi initiatives like L3Cube-MahaNLP \cite{joshi2022l3cube} provided classification encoders (MahaBERT) but lack generative sequence transduction capabilities.
Benchmarks like INDIC-DIALECT \cite{kumar2024indicdialect} evaluate Hindi regional varieties (Bhojpuri, Magahi, Maithili), while WFST hybrid systems \cite{pratap2024wfst} handle semiotic text normalization.
Prior Marathi research focused on speech/acoustic classification \cite{mulla2022text,pawar2026automatic}, read-speech transcripts such as RESPIN-S1.0 \cite{amartyaveer2025improving,kumar2025respin}, or zero-shot RAG prompting \cite{gaikwad2025marathi,vyavhare2026marathi}.
We bridge this gap by establishing the first 5-fold cross-validated parallel text normalization benchmark for regional Marathi sub-dialects.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Synthetic Data Generation and Multi-Tier Verification}}

LLM-driven synthetic data generation mitigates extreme data scarcity in low-resource settings.
Self-instruct mechanisms \cite{wang2022selfinstruct} and instruction tuning (Alpaca \cite{taori2023alpaca}, Orca \cite{mukherjee2023orca}) demonstrate that strong teacher models can distill capabilities into compact student transformers \cite{ho2023distilling,pecher2023better,degibert2024scaling}.
However, naive synthetic generation for unstandardized dialects risks language leakage, hallucinated entities, and uncorrected dialect residue.
We address this by combining domain-constrained 1-prompt-per-sentence generation via Gemma-4 \cite{gemma2024} with a multi-tier verification engine incorporating Devanagari script filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B \cite{dubey2024llama3} semantic auditing, ensuring verified parallel training corpora.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Target Dialects and Linguistic Properties}}

We target the following major Marathi dialects that span the primary regions of Maharashtra and exhibit distinct linguistic and phonetic patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Southern Konkan (Konkan Coast)}: Characterized by shortening of final-word vowels, softening of dental consonants, and different auxiliary verb forms. 
    Lexical differences include systematic vowel shifts: standard \emph{जातो} (``he goes'') becomes Southern Konkan \emph{जातां}; standard \emph{कुठे} (``where'') becomes \emph{कुठंय}. Domain-specific terminology generally remains untouched but often accompanied by Konkani words.
    \item \textbf{Northern Konkan (Khandesh Region)}: Heavily influenced by neighboring Gujarati and Bhili language families,thus it exhibits significant consonant simplification and dental consonant shifts. 
    Notabel transformations include: standard \emph{कशाला} (``why'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{कसाले}; standard \emph{मुलगा} (``boy'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{पोर}. Northern Konkan posseses the highest degree of lexical divergence among the three target dialects.
    \item \textbf{Varhadi (Vidarbha/Eastern Maharashtra)}: Spoken in Vidarbha, It exhibits characteristic differences and verb suffix modifications. Standard interrogative \emph{का?} (``why?'') becomes Varhadi \emph{काऊन?}; verbal forms undergo regular suffix mapping patterns. Among the three dialects, Varhadi maintains closest structural similarity to Standard Marathi as transformations are more systematic.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{LLM-Driven Parallel Corpus Generation and Verification}}

To deal with data scarcity, we developed an automated synthetic data generation pipeline powered by Gemma-4 \cite{gemma2024} in 9B-instruction-tuned (\texttt{gemma-4}) configurations, accessed via Ollama.
We considered several design decisions to ensure generation quality to be maximised:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1-Prompt-Per-Sentence Architecture}: Instead of generating batches of parallel pairs per API call (which risks array length mismatches and context contamination), each dialectal sentence is processed separately with a self-contained prompt.
    \item \textbf{Detailed Prompting Strategy}: The generation prompt enforces semantic fidelity rules: the standard output must preserve exact meaning, domain terminology (agriculture/banking), named entities, and numerical values from the input. The prompt includes dialect-specific lexical mapping tables and 5-8 few-shot examples per dialect.
    \item \textbf{Temperature Control}: Generation temperature is set to 0.3 with top-$p$ = 0.9 to balance diversity with fidelity.
\end{itemize}


All generated pairs undergo a three-tier verification and correction pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Rule-Based Syntactic Filter.} Automated validation checks implement Indic Abugida script normalization principles \cite{ansary2024unicode}, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Devanagari Script Integrity}: Both source and target must possess $>$90\% Devanagari Unicode characters within the \texttt{U+0900--U+097F} range, automatically discarding unnecessary characters.
        \item \emph{Length Ratio Constraint}: $0.5 \le |L_{\text{dialect}}| / |L_{\text{standard}}| \le 2.0$ to avoid hallucinations.
        \item \emph{Non-Identity Constraint}: $L_{\text{dialect}} \neq L_{\text{standard}}$ to exclude identical string copies.
        \item \emph{Minimum Token Count}: Both source and target must contain $\ge 3$ whitespace-separated tokens.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: LLM Semantic Verification.} A secondary LLM evaluator using \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant} (accessed via Groq API) performs semantic equivalence verification. The verifier rates on a 1-5 score scale considering: (a) semantic preservation; (b) absence of hallucinated entities; (c) absence of Hindi/English contamination; and (d) complete standardization of dialects. Pairs scoring $< 4.0$ are rejected or routed to correction.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Loop Correction Engine.} Pairs flagged by Tier 1 or Tier 2 enter a two-step correction loop: Step~1 generates corrected translations using specialised prompts highlighting the specific failure reason (e.g., ``Hindi leakage detected'' or ``Incomplete normalization''); Step~2 verifies the corrected sentences again,completely discarding pairs that fail a second audit pass.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Evaluation Protocol}}

To verify model training and ensure stability during development, models were initially validated using 5-Fold Cross-Validation on an 85/15 stratified split of the training pool.
For final benchmark reporting, models were trained on the complete dataset (combining original and synthetically expanded pairs) and evaluated on the official held-out RESPIN test set (\texttt{IISc\_RESPIN\_test\_mr}, comprising 2,170 utterances across target dialects).

This setup is applied across all dataset configurations: Southern Konkan (Original), Northern Konkan (Original), Varhadi (Original), Combined-data (Original), and their Synthetically Expanded counterparts.
All results are reported using standard automatic metrics computed via SacreBLEU \cite{post2018sacrebleu} and Word Error Rate (WER) scripts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy): BLEU-4 with 4-gram precision, brevity penalty, and smoothing method ``exp''.
    \item \textbf{chrF++}: Character n-gram F-score combined with word unigrams and bigrams, capturing subword morphological variations in Marathi text.
    \item \textbf{Normalized WER / CER}: Word Error Rate and Character Error Rate computed after Devanagari Unicode script standardization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Model Architecture Analysis}}

Table~\ref{tab:arch_compare} provides a detailed architectural comparison between the two primary models.
The 69.51 vs.\ 57.12 BLEU gap on the expanded multi-dialect task (+12.39 absolute) can be attributed to three key architectural differences:

\begin{table}[t]
\color{black}
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\caption{\textcolor{black}{Detailed Architecture Comparison: IndicBART (244M) vs.\ mT5-small (300M)}}
\label{tab:arch_compare}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXX}
\hline
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{IndicBART (244M)} & \textbf{mT5-small (300M)} \\
\hline
Base Architecture & mBART-50 (BART Enc-Dec) & T5 Encoder-Decoder \\
Position Encoding & Absolute sinusoidal & Relative positional bias \\
Vocabulary Size & 64,000 SentencePiece & 250,000 SentencePiece (mC4) \\
Pre-training Data & 11 Indic + English & 101 languages (mC4) \\

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vocabulary Coverage}: mT5's 250K vocabulary provides 3.9$\times$ more subword units than IndicBART's 64K, enabling finer-grained tokenization of morphologically complex Marathi dialectal forms. This is critical for dialect normalization, where subtle suffix and infix changes must be captured at the subword level.
    \item \textbf{Positional Encoding}: mT5 uses relative positional biases, which generalize better to variable-length sequences typical of dialectal text where sentence length may differ substantially between dialect and standard forms.
    \item \textbf{Conditioning Overhead}: IndicBART requires explicit language tokens (\texttt{<2mr>}) that add conditioning complexity, whereas mT5's pure text-to-text format enables direct mapping without task-specific architectural constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Conclusion}}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presents a comprehensive, reproducible framework for multi-dialect Marathi text normalization, addressing severe data scarcity through automated synthetic data expansion and establishing rigorous neural sequence-to-sequence benchmarks.

Our LLM-driven synthetic data augmentation pipeline, powered by Gemma-2 with multi-tier verification (Devanagari script filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B semantic auditing), successfully doubled the clean parallel corpus through synthetic expansion while maintaining strict semantic fidelity---a scalable paradigm for other low-resource dialect pairs.
Fine-tuning sequence-to-sequence transformers on the expanded benchmark established state-of-the-art performance: google/mT5-small (300M) achieved 69.51 BLEU and 84.58 chrF++ on the synthetically expanded multi-dialect dataset, outperforming IndicBART (244M) by +12.39 BLEU.
Individual dialect performance reached 80.99 BLEU and 91.21 chrF++ for Varhadi, demonstrating that compact pre-trained sequence-to-sequence transformers can achieve high-accuracy dialect normalization.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Limitations}}

This work has several limitations that should be acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Synthetic data generation relies on commercial LLM APIs (Gemma-2 via Google AI Studio), introducing API cost and rate limit considerations.
    \item While we benchmarked two encoder-decoder architectures (mT5-small and IndicBART), decoder-only LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-3, Gemma-2) have not been evaluated for zero-shot or fine-tuned text normalization.
    \item Evaluation is currently focused on text normalization metrics (BLEU, chrF++, WER); large-scale downstream task evaluation across diverse domain applications remains for future work.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Data and Code Availability}}

The complete parallel corpus of 32,335 verified dialect--Standard Marathi sentence pairs, along with all training code, evaluation scripts, and pre-trained model checkpoints, will be publicly released upon publication.
The dataset will be available under an open license to support reproducible research in Indic dialect normalization.
Our synthetic data generation pipeline, multi-tier verification engine, and fine-tuning configurations are documented in the accompanying code repository to enable replication and extension to additional Marathi sub-dialects and other low-resource Indic language varieties.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Future Directions}}

Several promising directions emerge from this work:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Edge Deployment via Quantization}: Model quantization (INT8/INT4) and knowledge distillation to sub-100M parameter models for on-device deployment in rural Maharashtra.
    \item \textbf{Expansion to Additional Dialects}: Extending the pipeline to Deshi/Puneri, Kolhapuri, Marathwada, and other Indic language sub-dialects.
    \item \textbf{Decoder-Only LLM Benchmarking}: Evaluating instruction-tuned decoder-only models for zero-shot and few-shot dialect normalization tasks.
\end{enumerate}
