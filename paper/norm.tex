\begin{abstract}
\textcolor{red}{Regional sub-dialects of Indic languages suffer from acute resource scarcity and an absence of aligned parallel corpora for generative text normalization. In this work, we present an end-to-end neural sequence-to-sequence framework for normalizing non-standard spoken transcripts from three regional Marathi varieties---Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi---into formal Standard Written Marathi. To address severe data constraints, we introduce an automated synthetic parallel expansion pipeline pairing 1-call-per-sentence Gemma-4 generation with a multi-tier verification engine comprising Devanagari Unicode rule filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B semantic auditing, expanding the parallel dataset from 16,163 to 32,335 verified pairs. We systematically evaluate IndicBART (244M) and mT5-small (300M) across single-dialect and multi-dialect configurations under stratified 5-fold cross-validation and the held-out RESPIN test benchmark (2,170 utterances). Experimental findings demonstrate that mT5-small achieves 69.51 BLEU and 84.58 chrF++ on the expanded multi-dialect benchmark, outperforming IndicBART by 12.39 BLEU. Crucially, multi-tier verification prevents synthetic quality collapse (+14.03 BLEU recovery over unverified data), establishing verified synthetic augmentation as an effective foundation for low-resource dialect normalization.}

\keywords{Dialect Normalization, Low-Resource NLP, Marathi Dialects, Sequence-to-Sequence Modelling, Synthetic Data Augmentation, mT5, IndicBART.}
\end{abstract}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Introduction}}

Rapid development of deep learning architectures and self-attention mechanisms \citep{vaswani2017attention} has made it possible to develop systems capable of understanding and generating natural language. Open-weight models have gained popularity to process text in various domains as they provide a finer control over system architecture, runtime environment, and training data \citep{dubey2024llama3}. Yet current language systems tend to work efficiently with standard high-resource languages. Queries based on non-standard input yield unexpected and undesired outputs \citep{joshi2020state}. Speakers of regional dialects face many issues in systems that use these models across automated speech recognition, machine translation and other language processing systems \citep{koenecke2020racial,zampieri2020nlp}.

Dialect normalization is the task of translating non-standard regional dialects into standard written language. The main challenge to be addressed while designing such systems is the preservation of the meaning of original text \citep{blaschke2024what}. It serves to be an essential layer for digital linguistic inclusion. Countries having large and diverse speech communities, such as India, which has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects spoken by 1.4 billion people, regional varieties have remained unattended in computational linguistic research. An example of such linguistic disparity is Marathi, an Indo-Aryan language spoken by over 83 million people in western India \citep{kulkarni2023variation}. Development of reliable pipelines for such regional Indic dialects have faced these fundamental challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Resource Scarcity}: Parallel corpora mapping the regional dialects to standard written language do not exist. Curation of such sets by experts fluent in multiple dialects is both expensive and cannot be scaled \citep{blaschke2024what,dhasmana2026dialect}.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Grammatical Variance}: Marathi exhibits rich morphology where different words undergo varied complex transformations across different regions where the dialects are spoken \citep{ansary2024unicode}\citep{joshi2025survey}.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Degraded performance of Multilingual Models}: Performance of pre-trained models like IndicBART \citep{dabre2022indicbart}, IndicNLP Suite \citep{kakwani2020indicnlpsuite}, and mT5 \citep{xue2021mt5} seems to drop drastically on non-standard language variants due to fragmentation during tokenisation and out-of-vocabulary phonological shifts \citep{joshi2020state,kumar2024indicdialect}.
\end{itemize}

In this work, we investigate the normalization performance of IndicBART and mt5 across regional Marathi dialects and evaluate how efficiently they adapt to dialect-to-standard mapping under task-specific conditioning, and check whether synthetic data can solve severe data while maintianing considerable performance. 
% To address these objectives, we construct an end-to-end normalization framework combining LLM-based synthetic generation, multi-tier syntactic and semantic verification, and conditioned transformer fine-tuning across both single-dialect and multi-dialect settings.}

Our core contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Parallel Corpus Generation}: Based on transcripts from the RESPIN dataset \citep{kumar2025respin}, we construct a parallel corpus for three of the Marathi dialects (Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi) mapped to Standard Marathi.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Automated Synthetic Augmentation with Verification}: We design a data expansion pipeline paired with a multi-tier verification engine, which has rule-based script filtration and LLM-based semantic inspection.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Regional Dialect Standardization across Language Families}}
Early approaches in the context of European languages used Character-Level Statistical Machine Translation (CSMT) and phrase-based models for Swiss German Dialects \citep{scherrer2016swiss} which were later replaced by pre-trained language models \citep{lusetti2018impact,kresic2024normalizing}. Similar approaches have been developed for Finnish, Swedish, and Estonian dialects \citep{partanen2019dialect,hamalainen2020normalizing,hamalainen2022generating}. A study of four language families by Kuparinen et al. \citep{kuparinen2023dialect} suggested that fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence transformers surpassed the rule-based and SMT baselines. Other than European countries, East Asian and African-Asian varieties have also shown similar patterns. Japanese regional dialects have been processed using phrase-level LSTM modules \citep{abe2018multi}, while Mandarin scripts relied on acoustic-phonetic mapping \citep{zhao2022mandarin}. The Conventional Orthography for Dialectal Arabic (CODA) framework \citep{habash2012coda} in the landscape of Arabic languages gave guidelines for Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) which led to multi-city translation benchmarks (MADAR \citep{bouamor2018madar}, NADI \citep{abdulmageed2023nadi}) and morphology-oriented prompt design for LLMs \citep{eryani2020arabic,dimakis2025dialect}.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Indic Dialect Landscape and Marathi NLP}}
Even though pre-trained models such as IndicBART \citep{dabre2022indicbart} and mT5 \citep{xue2021mt5}, which are trained on multilingual corpora cover major languages, they lack understanding of regional sub-dialects. Models based on a single language like L3Cube-MahaNLP \citep{joshi2022l3cube} offer pre-trained Marathi models for classification, but do not support generative text standardization. INDIC-DIALECT \citep{kumar2024indicdialect} considers Hindi varieties (Bhojpuri, Magahi, Maithili), for evaluation via WFST frameworks for text normalization while preserving the underlying semantics \citep{pratap2024wfst}. However, Marathi dialects were primarily dealt for acoustic/ASR classification \citep{mulla2022text,pawar2026automatic}, or zero-shot LLM retrieval \citep{gaikwad2025marathi,vyavhare2026marathi}. Our work fills this gap by presenting an end-to-end neural fine-tuning pipeline with evaluations and data augmentation strategies.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{LLM Distillation and Multi-Tier Verification}}
LLMs can be used to suffice for data scarcity in various tasks \citep{wang2022selfinstruct,taori2023alpaca,mukherjee2023orca}. Knowledge distillation strategies can be employed to train compact and efficient models based on the outputs generated by a larger teacher model \citep{ho2023distilling,pecher2023better,degibert2024scaling}. But the use of such techniques for low-resource regional dialects involves significant amount of hallucinations and noise \citep{degibert2024scaling,ji2023survey}. We solve this by utilising Gemma-4 \citep{gemma2024} with a robust morphologically aware system prompt and a multi-tier verification system that ensures integrity of the data generated.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Target Dialects and Linguistic Properties}}
We target the following Marathi dialects based on the RESPIN corpus:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Southern Konkan (Konkan Coast)}: Characterized by shortening of final-word vowels, softening of dental consonants, and different auxiliary verb forms.  differences include systematic vowel shifts: standard \emph{जातो} (``he goes'') becomes Southern Konkan \emph{जातां}; standard \emph{कुठे} (``where'') becomes \emph{कुठंय}. 
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Northern Konkan (Khandesh Region)}: Heavily influenced by neighbouring Gujarati and Bhili language families, thus it exhibits significant consonant simplification and dental consonant shifts. Notable transformations include: standard \emph{कशाला} (``why'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{कसाले}; standard \emph{मुलगा} (``boy'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{पोर}. Northern Konkan possesses the highest degree of lexical divergence among the three target dialects.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Varhadi (Vidarbha/Eastern Maharashtra)}: Spoken in Vidarbha, it exhibits characteristic differences and verb suffix modifications. Standard interrogative \emph{का?} (``why?'') becomes Varhadi \emph{काऊन?}; verbal forms undergo regular suffix mapping patterns. Among the three dialects, Varhadi maintains closest structural similarity to Standard Marathi as transformations are more systematic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Synthetic Parallel Data Generation Pipeline}}

To deal with data scarcity, we developed an automated synthetic data translation and generation pipeline powered by Gemma-4 \citep{gemma2024} with 12B parameters configuration, accessed via Ollama. The complete end-to-end normalization architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}. We considered several design decisions to ensure generation quality to be maximised:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape 1-Call-Per-Sentence Generation}: We observed batch generation caused hallucination and sentence alignment errors across dialect boundaries. Therefore, we used a 1-call-per-sentence translation regime.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Preservation of Meaning and Domain Constraints}: System prompts strictly instructed the model that while colloquial grammatical markers and phonetic spellings must be transformed into standard written Marathi, the standard output must preserve exact meaning, domain terminology (agriculture/banking), and named entities without creative embellishment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Verification and Quality Filtering Engine}}

LLMs can generate hallucinated texts, script corruption, or flawed target sequences when dealing with regional sub-dialects. We implemented a two-tier verification engine to purge flawed sentence pairs before model training:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Tier 1: Rule-Based Syntactic Filter.} Automated validation checks implement Indic Abugida script normalization principles \citep{ansary2024unicode}, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Script Consistency}: Discards samples containing non-Devanagari characters (e.g., Latin script, Chinese glyphs, stray punctuation artifacts).
        \item \emph{Length Ratio Constraint}: Discards pairs where sentence length ratio $\frac{\text{len}(\text{Target})}{\text{len}(\text{Source})} \notin [0.5, 2.0]$.
        \item \emph{Minimum Token Count}: Both source and target must contain $\ge 3$ whitespace-separated tokens.
    \end{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Tier 2: LLM Semantic Verification.} A secondary LLM evaluator using \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant} \citep{dubey2024llama3} grades translation accuracy on a 1--5 semantic scale under strict prompt guidelines. Pairs with semantic scores $< 3$ are purged.
\end{itemize}

Through this multi-tier verification process, 3,751 low-quality sentence pairs were purged from the unverified corpus (19,914 pairs), preventing error propagation into downstream models. The dataset composition across original, synthetic, and final clean partitions is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_composition}.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Model Fine-Tuning and Conditioning}}

We benchmark two pre-trained sequence-to-sequence architectures across both single-dialect and multi-dialect configurations: IndicBART (244M) \citep{dabre2022indicbart} and mT5-small (300M) \citep{xue2021mt5}.

To enable unified multi-dialect training where a single model normalizes all regional variants, we implement special token conditioning prefixes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Task Normalization Prefixes}: A normalisation tag (\texttt{<normalize>}) is attached that instructs the encoder to perform the dialect-to-standard text transformation.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Dialect-Source Identification Tags}: Source sentences are prefixed with dialect markers, \texttt{<D1>} for Southern Konkan, \texttt{<D2>} for Northern Konkan, and \texttt{<D4>} for Varhadi for explicitly conditioning the model particularly in the multi-dialect training scenario.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Target Standardization Tags}: Decoder target conditioning is done using standard language tags (\texttt{<standard\_mr>}), enforcing the output alignment into formal Standard Written Marathi.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Evaluation Protocol}}

\textcolor{red}{We report model performance across two distinct evaluation settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Mean 5-Fold Cross-Validation}: To evaluate model stability and in-domain generalization across the curated parallel datasets, models are trained and evaluated under an 85/15 stratified 5-fold cross-validation scheme (reported in Section~\ref{subsec:cv_findings}--\ref{subsec:ablation_findings}, Tables~\ref{tab:results_original_single}--\ref{tab:verification_ablation}, and Figures~\ref{fig:single_dialect_cv}--\ref{fig:verification_ablation}).
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Held-Out RESPIN Test Benchmark}: To measure true zero-shot/unseen generalization, models trained on the complete dataset are evaluated on the official held-out benchmark set (\texttt{IISc\_RESPIN\_test\_mr}), consisting of 2,170 unseen utterances across all target sub-dialects (reported in Section~\ref{subsec:heldout_findings}, Table~\ref{tab:respin_comprehensive}, and Figure~\ref{fig:respin_comprehensive}).
\end{itemize}}

Results are reported using metrics that are evaluated using SacreBLEU \citep{post2018sacrebleu} and Word Error Rate (WER) scripts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy): BLEU-4 with 4-gram precision, brevity penalty, with an exponential smoothing method.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape chrF++}: Character n-gram F-score combined with word unigrams and bigrams.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Normalized WER / CER}: Word Error Rate and Character Error Rate after Devanagari Unicode script standardization.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Conclusion}}

This study presents a scalable, reproducible framework for multi-dialect Marathi text normalization, addressing the critical scarcity of parallel corpora for low-resource Indic varieties. By replacing expensive manual annotations with a synthetic parallel generation pipeline combining Gemma-4 translation with a multi-tier verification engine (rule-based script filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B semantic auditing), we successfully expanded the clean parallel corpus from 16,163 to 32,335 verified pairs across Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi dialects. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that mT5-small achieves 69.51 BLEU and 84.58 chrF++ on the expanded multi-dialect benchmark, outperforming IndicBART by 12.39 BLEU. Crucially, multi-tier verification prevented synthetic quality collapse (+14.03 BLEU recovery over unverified data), while dataset expansion mitigated cross-dialect parameter competition and maintained near-perfect Standard Marathi normalization (97.39 BLEU, 1.37\% WER) on held-out RESPIN benchmarks. While these findings establish a strong blueprint for Indic sub-dialect normalization, minor constraints remain: LLM-driven verification pipelines introduce computational and latency overheads, synthetic expansion can occasionally yield slight precision dilution on already high-performing dialects like Varhadi, and intrinsic text metrics should be complemented by downstream task evaluations (e.g., ASR post-processing) in future work. Overall, our verified synthetic pipeline provides a scalable foundation for expanding digital linguistic inclusion across low-resource regional dialects.
