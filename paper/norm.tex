\begin{center}
{\fontsize{14pt}{18pt}\selectfont\bfseries From Regional Dialects to Standard Written Marathi: A Normalization pipeline based on sequence-to-sequence fine-tuning}\\[14pt]
{\fontsize{12pt}{16pt}\selectfont\bfseries Aditya Kinjawadekar\textsuperscript{1}, Surender Kannaiyan\textsuperscript{1}, and Saugata Sinha\textsuperscript{1}}\\[10pt]
{\fontsize{11pt}{15pt}\selectfont \textsuperscript{1}Visvesvaraya National Institute of Technology, Nagpur, India}\\[4pt]
{\fontsize{11pt}{15pt}\selectfont E-mail: kinjawadekaradi112@gmail.com}
\end{center}

\vspace{12pt}

\begin{abstract}
Scarcity of resources and absence of benchmarks for regional dialects of Indic languages is an established problem.
In this work, we present an end-to-end neural fine-tuning pipeline for normalization of dialectal transcripts from the Marathi dialects of Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi into Standard Marathi. 
To address the issues regarding data, we introduce a synthetic data generation pipeline with a multi-tier verification engine for generating the aligned corpus and expanding it further. 
We evaluate IndicBART (244M) and mT5-small (300M) across varied configurations. 
\textcolor{red}{Qualitative analysis of deterministic regex and dictionary rules reveals critical morphological and agreement failure modes, demonstrating the necessity of sequence-to-sequence neural architectures.} 
Experimental metrics on the held-out test benchmark show that mT5-small achieves 66.57 BLEU macro-average across the regional dialects under multi-dialect training (and 74.28 BLEU across the full 2,170-utterance test suite), outperforming IndicBART (51.67 BLEU macro / 57.50 BLEU full) by 14.90 BLEU, while IndicBART achieves its peak multi-dialect synthetic gain of +17.58 BLEU on Southern Konkan. 
Also, the multi-tier verification prevents severe quality degradation (+14.03 BLEU recovery over unverified data in cross-validation and +17.26 BLEU on the Southern Konkan test set), demonstrating the necessity of systematic filtering during synthetic augmentation for dialect normalization.

\keywords{Dialect Normalization, Low-Resource NLP, Marathi Dialects, Sequence-to-Sequence Modelling, Synthetic Data Augmentation, mT5, IndicBART.}
\end{abstract}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Introduction}}

Rapid development of deep learning architectures and self-attention mechanisms \citep{vaswani2017attention} has made it possible to develop systems capable of understanding and generating natural language. Open-weight models have gained popularity to process text in various domains as they provide a finer control over system architecture, runtime environment, and training data \citep{dubey2024llama3}. Yet current language systems tend to work efficiently with standard high-resource languages. Queries based on non-standard input yield unexpected and undesired outputs \citep{joshi2020state}. Speakers of regional dialects face many issues in systems that use these models across automated speech recognition, machine translation and other language processing systems \citep{koenecke2020racial,zampieri2020nlp}.

Dialect normalization is the task of translating non-standard regional dialects into standard written language. The main challenge to be addressed while designing such systems is the preservation of the meaning of original text \citep{blaschke2024what}. It serves to be an essential layer for digital linguistic inclusion. In countries having large and diverse speech communities, such as India, which has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects spoken by 1.4 billion people, regional varieties have remained unattended in computational linguistic research. An example of such linguistic disparity is Marathi, an Indo-Aryan language spoken by over 83 million people in western India \citep{kulkarni2023variation}. Development of reliable pipelines for such regional Indic dialects has faced these fundamental challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Resource Scarcity}: Parallel corpora mapping the regional dialects to standard written language do not exist. Curation of such sets by experts fluent in multiple dialects is both expensive and cannot be scaled \citep{blaschke2024what,dhasmana2026dialect}.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Grammatical Variance}: Marathi exhibits rich morphology where different words undergo varied complex transformations across different regions where the dialects are spoken \citep{ansary2024unicode,joshi2025survey}.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Degraded performance of Multilingual Models}: Performance of pre-trained models like IndicBART \citep{dabre2022indicbart}, IndicNLP Suite \citep{kakwani2020indicnlpsuite}, and mT5 \citep{xue2021mt5} seems to drop drastically on non-standard language variants due to fragmentation during tokenisation and out-of-vocabulary phonological shifts \citep{joshi2020state,kumar2024indicdialect}.
\end{itemize}

The dataset itself is based on agriculture and banking precisely because these represent critical domains where rural dialect speakers actually interact with digital services like agricultural advisory services, banking based services, and public grievance portals \citep{kumar2025respin}. 
Thus, standardization pipelines help chatbots and automated systems correctly understand user queries, even if the regional grammar differs \citep{blaschke2024what,joshi2025survey}. 
Normalization is also vital for search and information retrieval on public portals, where specific terms from the dialects must be mapped onto standard texts so that accurate retrieval is possible \citep{kumar2024indicdialect}. 
It readily supports rural document digitization by converting dialectal submissions into standardized, searchable archives. 
This pipeline addresses the barrier of exclusion in terms of computational research and digital inclusion by bridging the gaps between dialects and standard language without users needing to alter their native dialect or companies to rebuild their entire technology stack \citep{joshi2020state}.

In this work, we investigate the normalization performance of IndicBART and mT5 across regional Marathi dialects and evaluate how efficiently they adapt to dialect-to-standard mapping under task-specific conditioning, and check whether synthetic data can solve severe data scarcity while maintaining considerable performance. 

Our core contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Parallel Corpus Generation}: Based on transcripts from the RESPIN dataset \citep{kumar2025respin}, we construct a parallel corpus for three of the Marathi dialects (Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi) mapped to Standard Marathi.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Automated Synthetic Augmentation with Verification}: We design a data expansion pipeline paired with a multi-tier verification engine, which has rule-based script filtration and LLM-based semantic inspection.
    \item \textcolor{red}{{\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Deterministic Baseline Analysis}: We analyze deterministic regex and dictionary lookup baselines, isolating key structural failure mechanisms (case, agreement, contraction, and stem corruption) that motivate neural seq2seq modeling.}
    \item \textcolor{red}{{\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Multi-Dialect Neural Benchmarking}: We comprehensively benchmark IndicBART and mT5-small under single- and multi-dialect regimes across 5-fold cross-validation and the official RESPIN test suite (1,615 regional / 2,170 full utterances).}
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Regional Dialect Standardization across Language Families}}
Early approaches in the context of European languages used Character-Level Statistical Machine Translation (CSMT) and phrase-based models for Swiss German Dialects \citep{scherrer2016swiss} which were later replaced by pre-trained language models \citep{lusetti2018impact,kresic2024normalizing}. Similar approaches have been developed for Finnish, Swedish, and Estonian dialects \citep{partanen2019dialect,hamalainen2020normalizing,hamalainen2022generating}. A study of four language families by Kuparinen et al. \citep{kuparinen2023dialect} suggested that fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence transformers surpassed the rule-based and SMT baselines. Other than European countries, East Asian and African-Asian varieties have also shown similar patterns. Japanese regional dialects have been processed using phrase-level LSTM modules \citep{abe2018multi}, while Mandarin scripts relied on acoustic-phonetic mapping \citep{zhao2022mandarin}. The Conventional Orthography for Dialectal Arabic (CODA) framework \citep{habash2012coda} in the landscape of Arabic languages gave guidelines for Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) which led to multi-city translation benchmarks (MADAR \citep{bouamor2018madar}, NADI \citep{abdulmageed2023nadi}) and morphology-oriented prompt design for LLMs \citep{eryani2020arabic,dimakis2025dialect}.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Indic Dialect Landscape and Marathi NLP}}
Even though pre-trained models such as IndicBART \citep{dabre2022indicbart} and mT5 \citep{xue2021mt5}, which are trained on multilingual corpora cover major languages, they lack understanding of regional sub-dialects. Models based on a single language like L3Cube-MahaNLP \citep{joshi2022l3cube} offer pre-trained Marathi models for classification, but do not support generative text standardization. INDIC-DIALECT \citep{kumar2024indicdialect} considers Hindi varieties (Bhojpuri, Magahi, Maithili), for evaluation via WFST frameworks for text normalization while preserving the underlying semantics \citep{pratap2024wfst}. However, Marathi dialects were primarily dealt for acoustic/ASR classification \citep{mulla2022text,pawar2026automatic}, or zero-shot LLM retrieval \citep{gaikwad2025marathi,vyavhare2026marathi}. Our work fills this gap by presenting an end-to-end neural fine-tuning pipeline with evaluations and data augmentation strategies.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{LLM Distillation and Multi-Tier Verification}}
LLMs can be used to suffice for data scarcity in various tasks \citep{wang2022selfinstruct,taori2023alpaca,mukherjee2023orca}. Knowledge distillation strategies can be employed to train compact and efficient models based on the outputs generated by a larger teacher model \citep{ho2023distilling,pecher2023better,degibert2024scaling}. But the use of such techniques for low-resource regional dialects involves significant amount of hallucinations and noise \citep{degibert2024scaling,ji2023survey}. We solve this by utilising Gemma-4 \citep{gemma2024} with a robust morphologically aware system prompt and a multi-tier verification system that ensures integrity of the data generated.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Target Dialects and Linguistic Properties}}
We target the following Marathi dialects based on the RESPIN corpus:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Southern Konkan}: Known for its shortening of final-word vowels, softening of dental consonants, and different auxiliary verb forms. Differences include systematic vowel shifts: standard \emph{जातो} (``he goes'') becomes Southern Konkan \emph{जातां}; standard \emph{कुठे} (``where'') becomes \emph{कुठंय}. 
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Northern Konkan}: Heavily influenced by neighbouring Gujarati and Bhili language families, thus it has consonant simplification and dental consonant shifts. Some variations include: standard \emph{कशाला} (``why'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{कसाले}; standard \emph{मुलगा} (``boy'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{पोर}.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Varhadi}: Spoken in the regions of Vidarbha, it shows very specific verb suffix modifications. Standard interrogative \emph{का?} (``why?'') becomes Varhadi \emph{काऊन?}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Exploration of Deterministic Rule-Based Normalization and Linguistic Failure Modes}}
\label{subsec:rule_baseline_intro}

\color{red}
Prior to developing neural sequence-to-sequence fine-tuning pipelines, we evaluated deterministic heuristic and regular expression-based baselines to understand the scope and limitations of rule-based dialect normalization for Marathi.

\subsubsection{\textcolor{red}{Deterministic Rule Engineering}}
The deterministic baseline was constructed around linguistic rules spanning three levels of abstraction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Phonological and Orthographic Substitutions}: Regular expressions targeting systematic phonological alternations and vowel shortenings, including Southern Konkan word-final vowel elisions (\emph{-तां} $\rightarrow$ \emph{-तो}), Northern Konkan dental-sibilant variations (\emph{स} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{श}, \emph{च} $\leftrightarrow$ \emph{स}), and Varhadi diphthong compressions.
    \item \emph{Morphological Suffix and Postposition Dictionaries}: Direct replacement mappings for grammatical markers across the three dialects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Southern Konkan (Malvani): Genitive inflection substitutions (\emph{-चो/-ची/-चे} $\rightarrow$ \emph{-चा/-ची/-चे}), locative postpositions, and dialectal habitual aspect markers.
        \item Northern Konkan (Ahirani): Locative marker replacements (\emph{-मा/-म्हा} $\rightarrow$ \emph{-मध्ये}), genitive substitutions (\emph{-ना/-नी} $\rightarrow$ \emph{-चा/-ची}), and auxiliary verb shifts (\emph{शे/शात} $\rightarrow$ \emph{आहे/आहेत}).
        \item Varhadi: Dative pronoun replacements (\emph{मले} $\rightarrow$ \emph{मला}, \emph{तुले} $\rightarrow$ \emph{तुला}), interrogative markers (\emph{काऊन} $\rightarrow$ \emph{का}), and past auxiliary shifts (\emph{व्हता/व्हती} $\rightarrow$ \emph{होता/होती}).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \emph{Lexical Replacement Dictionaries}: Word-level bilingual substitution lexicons for frequent dialect-specific lexical items (e.g., Ahirani \emph{पोर} $\rightarrow$ \emph{मुलगा}, \emph{कसाले} $\rightarrow$ \emph{कशाला}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\textcolor{red}{Linguistic Failure Modes and Observations}}
Linguistic analysis of the resulting text revealed that deterministic surface replacement fails to produce valid Standard Written Marathi due to five fundamental structural challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context-Free Agreement Failures}: Surface rules perform blind token replacements without verifying clause-level gender-number-person agreement. For example, in Southern Konkan \emph{सगळ्या प्रकारच्या मातयेचो कस सारखो नसता}, replacing the genitive \emph{मातयेचो} $\rightarrow$ \emph{मातीचा} succeeds locally, but the rule fails to inflect the habitual verb \emph{नसता} to masculine singular \emph{नसतो}, leaving the ungrammatical output *\emph{...कस सारखा नसता}.
    \item \textbf{Infinitive and Purposive Contraction Breakdown}: Dialectal compound postpositional phrases such as Malvani \emph{घेऊच्या खाती} require holistic grammatical restructuring into standard purposive infinitival forms (\emph{घेण्यासाठी}). Token-by-token replacement generates ungrammatical intermediate sequences (*\emph{घेऊच्या साठी}).
    \item \textbf{Oblique Stem and Clause Inversion}: Non-canonical syntactic structures, such as Ahirani \emph{करनी चोरी करान सरकार भी नजर म्हा गुन्हा समजावस}, require argument realignment and passive voice reconstruction (\emph{सरकारच्या नजरेत गुन्हा समजला जातो}). Naive dictionary matching leaves the unparsed dialectal case chain corrupted.
    \item \textbf{Lexical Polysemy and Contextual Shifts}: Dialectal content words and adverbs (e.g., Ahirani \emph{आल्लग} meaning ``different/separate'', \emph{वरीसनं} meaning ``annual'') lack simple 1-to-1 surface translations and require sentence context to identify standard equivalents (\emph{वेगवेगळी}, \emph{वार्षिक}).
    \item \textbf{Agglutinative Oblique Suffix Corruption}: Appending standard case markers to uninflected dialectal noun bases creates ungrammatical hybrid stems. For example, in Varhadi \emph{बईनीले} (standard: \emph{बहिणीला}), replacing the dative marker \emph{-ले} with \emph{-ला} on the raw stem produces invalid *\emph{बहीणला} instead of correctly forming the standard oblique stem \emph{बहिणी-}.
\end{enumerate}

These qualitative observations demonstrate that dialect normalization is fundamentally a sequence-to-sequence syntactic translation task rather than a surface token-substitution task, motivating our neural fine-tuning approach.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Synthetic Parallel Data Generation Pipeline}}

To deal with data scarcity, we developed an automated synthetic data translation and generation pipeline powered by Gemma-4 \citep{gemma2024} with 12B parameters configuration, accessed via Ollama. The complete end-to-end normalization architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}. We considered several design decisions to ensure generation quality to be maximised:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape 1-Call-Per-Sentence Generation}: We observed batch generation caused hallucinations in context. Therefore, a single API call was utilized for a single sentence, flushing the KV-cache at every instant that gave fresh context to the LLM every time.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Preservation of Meaning}: System prompts were designed in a way that actually instructed the model that while the dialectal text is to be translated into standard written Marathi, the standard output must preserve the exact semantic meaning, domain-specific terminology (agriculture/banking), and proper nouns without any change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Verification and Quality Filtering Engine}}

LLMs can generate flawed target sequences when dealing with regional sub-dialects. 
We implemented a two-tier verification engine to remove them before model training:

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Tier 1: Rule-Based Syntactic Filter.} Automated validation checks implement Indic Abugida script normalization principles \citep{ansary2024unicode}, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Script Consistency}: Discards samples containing non-Devanagari characters.
        \item \emph{Length Ratio Constraint}: Discards pairs where sentence length ratio $\frac{\text{len}(\text{Target})}{\text{len}(\text{Source})} \notin [0.5, 2.0]$.
        \item \emph{Minimum Token Count}: Both source and target must contain $\ge 3$ whitespace-separated tokens.
    \end{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Tier 2: LLM Semantic Verification.} A secondary LLM evaluator using \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant} \citep{dubey2024llama3} evaluates the accuracy on a scale of 1-5 under redesigned system prompts. 
    Pairs with semantic scores $< 3$ are discarded.
\end{itemize}

Through this two-step verification process, flawed and hallucinated translations were systematically filtered out (with an empirical rejection rate of 4.83\%, discarding 821 low-quality pairs from 16,993 candidate generations), preventing error propagation into downstream models. 
The verified clean pairs (16,172 pairs) were merged with the baseline data, successfully expanding the aligned parallel dataset from 16,163 original pairs to 32,335 verified clean pairs across all target dialects.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Model Fine-Tuning and Conditioning}}

We evaluate two pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models across different configurations: IndicBART (244M) \citep{dabre2022indicbart} and mT5-small (300M) \citep{xue2021mt5}.

To enable unified multi-dialect training where a single model is used for all regional variants, we design special task-based conditioning prefixes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Task Normalization Prefixes}: A normalisation tag (\texttt{<normalize>}) is attached that instructs the encoder to perform the dialect-to-standard text transformation.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Dialect-Source Identification Tags}: Source sentences have dialect markers, \texttt{<D1>} for Southern Konkan, \texttt{<D2>} for Northern Konkan, and \texttt{<D4>} for Varhadi for explicit conditioning of the models in multi-dialect training and testing scenarios.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Target Standardization Tags}: Decoder target is conditioned using standard language tags (\texttt{<standard\_mr>}), enforcing the output alignment into Standard Written Marathi.
\end{itemize}

These inherent structural advantages allow mT5-small to scale its performance in multi-dialect scenarios.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Evaluation Protocol}}

We report the model performance across the following two evaluation settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Mean 5-Fold Cross-Validation}: To evaluate the stability of models during training, they are trained and evaluated under an 85/15 stratified 5-fold cross-validation scheme.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape Held-Out RESPIN Test Benchmark}: To evaluate performance on unseen data, models were trained on the complete dataset and later evaluated on the official held-out test directory (\texttt{IISc\_RESPIN\_test\_mr}), consisting of 2,170 spoken utterances in total: Southern Konkan (D1, 559 utterances), Northern Konkan (D2, 540 utterances), Standard Written Marathi (D3, 555 utterances), and Varhadi (D4, 516 utterances). Because our task is dialect normalization, primary evaluation focuses on the 1,615 regional dialectal utterances (D1, D2, and D4), while the standard dialect subset (D3, 555 utterances) serves as an in-domain identity control to verify non-degradation on already standard text.
\end{itemize}

Results are reported using standard text normalization evaluation metrics via SacreBLEU \citep{post2018sacrebleu}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy): BLEU-4 with 4-gram precision, brevity penalty, with an exponential smoothing method.
    \item {\fontsize{11pt}{14pt}\selectfont\itshape chrF++}: Character n-gram F-score combined with word unigrams and bigrams, capturing subword morphological variations in Devanagari.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Conclusion}}

This study builds and presents a reproducible framework for text normalization across three Marathi dialects. 
By the efficient use of LLMs, it solves the problem of not having enough parallel data for low-resource Indic language varieties instead of relying on manual annotations.
Also, the verification engine proves to be effective since its correction ability prevented the models from performance degradation.
\textcolor{red}{Additionally, our qualitative error analysis demonstrates the inadequacy of deterministic regex and dictionary lookup rules on complex Indic morphosyntax, proving that sequence-to-sequence neural fine-tuning is indispensable for dialect normalization.}

Our experiments on the held-out test benchmark demonstrate that mT5-small achieves 66.57 BLEU macro-average on the expanded multi-dialect dataset (74.28 BLEU on the full 2,170-utterance test suite), outperforming IndicBART (51.67 BLEU macro / 57.50 BLEU full), with strong individual dialect performance reaching 78.88 BLEU on Northern Konkan and 76.00 BLEU on Varhadi. IndicBART shows significant benefit from synthetic expansion on Southern Konkan (+17.58 BLEU gain). Furthermore, our multi-tier verification engine proves essential in filtering out flawed synthetic pairs, preventing error propagation and recovering +14.03 BLEU for IndicBART in cross-validation (+17.26 BLEU on test D1).

Ultimately, this system behaves as a middleware for public and financial digital services that allows regional speakers to interact naturally in their dialects without a need for institutions to rebuild standard Indic NLP backends.

These results offer a decent starting point for normalizing other Indic sub-dialects, but a few limitations remain. 
Firstly, LLMs add up to the extra cost of infrastructure and compute. 
Also, synthetic expansion comes at a cost of degraded performance for dialects that already perform well (Varhadi dialect in this case).
Future work should test the performance around speech-based systems, not just text-based metrics. 
Overall, our verified pipeline offers a scalable way to expand digital language support for low-resource regional dialects.
