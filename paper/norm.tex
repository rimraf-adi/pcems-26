\begin{abstract}
\textcolor{blue}{Low-resource regional dialects pose severe challenges for Natural Language Processing (NLP) systems due to pronounced morpho-syntactic variation, non-standard orthography, and extreme scarcity of parallel corpora. While dialect normalization benchmarks exist for European and Arabic language varieties, regional Indic sub-dialects remain virtually unbenchmarked owing to the high cost of manual expert annotation. In this paper, we address dialect normalization for Marathi---converting non-standard regional dialectal text into Standard Written Marathi---focusing on three major regional dialects of Maharashtra: Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi. To overcome data scarcity without costly human annotation, we introduce an automated synthetic parallel data expansion pipeline combining Gemma-4 generation with a multi-tier verification engine (rule-based script filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B semantic auditing), doubling the clean parallel training corpus to 32,335 verified pairs. Sequence-to-sequence transformer architectures---google/mT5-small (300M) and IndicBART (244M)---are fine-tuned using task-specific normalization conditioning tags (\texttt{<normalize>}, dialect markers \texttt{<D1>}/\texttt{<D2>}/\texttt{<D4>}, and target standardization tags \texttt{<standard\_mr>}) under a 5-fold cross-validation scheme. Empirical results demonstrate that mT5-small achieves state-of-the-art performance with 69.51 BLEU and 84.58 chrF++ on the expanded multi-dialect dataset (+12.39 BLEU over IndicBART), with individual dialect performance reaching 81.00 BLEU on Varhadi. Crucially, multi-tier verification prevents synthetic quality collapse (+14.03 BLEU recovery over unverified data), while dataset expansion eliminates cross-dialect parameter interference and maintains near-perfect Standard Marathi normalization (97.39 BLEU, 1.37\% WER) on held-out RESPIN benchmarks.}

\keywords{Dialect Normalization, Low-Resource NLP, Marathi Dialects, Sequence-to-Sequence Modeling, Synthetic Data Augmentation, Task-Specific Conditioning, mT5, IndicBART.}
\end{abstract}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Introduction}}


Rapid development of deep learning architectures and self-attention mechanisms~\cite{vaswani2017attention} has made it possible to develop systems capable of understanding and generating natural language.
Open-weight models have gained popularity to process text in various domains because they provide a finer control over model architecture, runtime environment, and training data~\cite{dubey2024llama3}.
Yet current language systems tend to give preference to standard high-resource languages. Queries based on non-standard input yield unexpected and undesired outputs. ~\cite{joshi2020state}.
Speakers of regional dialects encounter severe drops in performance of systems that use these models across automated speech recognition, machine translation, and text processing due to lexical, phonetic, and complex morphological variations~\cite{koenecke2020racial,blaschke2024what,joshi2025survey}.

Dialect normalization is the task of translating non-standard regional dialects into standard written language.
The main challenge in designing such systems is to preserve the meaning of original sentence. ~\cite{blaschke2024what}.
It is an essential layer for digital linguistic inclusion. Countries having large and diverse speech communities, such as India, which has 22 official languages and hundreds of regional dialects spoken by 1.4 billion people, regional varieties tend to remain unattended in computational linguistic research.
An example of such linguistic disparity is Marathi, an Indo-Aryan language spoken by over 83 million people in western India~\cite{kulkarni2023variation}.

Building pipelines for translating Indic dialects primarily comes down to two core challenges:
\textit{Extreme Data Scarcity}: Datasets that map regional dialects to standard forms are technically non-existent; and
\textit{Morphological Complexity}: Marathi is a language with rich morphology, where noun cases, postpositional affixes, and verbal tense suffixes usually show complex variations~\cite{kulkarni2023variation,ansary2024unicode}.

Evaluations on different dialectal texts could show discrepancies due to variations across regional Marathi dialects~\cite{kulkarni2023variation,joshi2025survey}. Although dialect normalization benchmarks exist for certain European and Arabic dialects~\cite{samardzic2016archimob,habash2012coda,bouamor2018madar,kuparinen2023dialect}, Marathi-based benchmarks remain limited due to resource scarcity~\cite{joshi2020state,gavali2026marathi,gaikwad2025marathi}. This problem of data scarcity in niche domains has traditionally been solved through manual annotations via experts, relying on a process that is time-consuming and expensive to manage~\cite{blaschke2024what}.

We propose a framework to solve these problems. We design a normalization pipeline for Marathi dialects that converts the dialectal text into standard Marathi text by fine-tuning pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models, benchmarking the performance of the pipeline with synthetic data augmentation. 

Our core contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parallel Corpus Generation}: Based on transcripts from the RESPIN dataset~\cite{kumar2025respin}, we construct an aligned parallel corpus for three major Marathi dialects (Southern Konkan, Northern Konkan, and Varhadi) mapped to Standard Marathi across agricultural and banking domains.
    \item \textbf{Automated Synthetic Augmentation with Verification}: We design a synthetic data expansion pipeline using Gemma-4~\cite{gemma2024} paired with a multi-tier verification engine (rule-based script filtering and LLM semantic auditing), demonstrating that audited synthetic data significantly improves downstream model accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Related Work}}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Regional Dialect Standardization across Language Families}}
Early work in European dialect normalization relied on Character-Level Statistical Machine Translation (CSMT) and phrase-based SMT for Swiss German Dialects~\cite{scherrer2016swiss}, which were subsequently superseded by pre-trained language models and neural sequence-to-sequence architectures~\cite{lusetti2018impact,kresic2024normalizing}.
Parallel efforts have addressed dialectal normalization across Uralic and Scandinavian varieties, including Finnish, Swedish, and Estonian~\cite{partanen2019dialect,hamalainen2020normalizing,hamalainen2022generating}.
A systematic multi-lingual evaluation across four language families by Kuparinen et al.~\cite{kuparinen2023dialect} demonstrated that fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence transformers consistently outperform rule-based and statistical baselines.
Beyond European languages, East Asian and Afro-Asiatic dialect processing has evolved along similar lines: Japanese regional dialects have been modeled via phrase-level LSTMs~\cite{abe2018multi}, Mandarin non-standard varieties via acoustic-phonetic mapping~\cite{zhao2022mandarin}, and Dialectal Arabic via the Conventional Orthography for Dialectal Arabic (CODA) framework~\cite{habash2012coda}, enabling multi-city translation benchmarks (MADAR~\cite{bouamor2018madar}, NADI~\cite{abdulmageed2023nadi}) and morphology-aware prompting strategies~\cite{eryani2020arabic,dimakis2025dialect}.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Indic Dialect Landscape and Marathi NLP}}

While pre-trained multilingual language models such as IndicBART~\cite{dabre2022indicbart}, IndicNLP Suite~\cite{kakwani2020indicnlpsuite}, and mT5~\cite{xue2021mt5} cover major Indic national languages, they suffer from significant performance degradation on unscripted regional sub-dialects.
Monolingual suites like L3Cube-MahaNLP~\cite{joshi2022l3cube} provide pre-trained Marathi models for classification and tagging, but lack sequence transduction capabilities for generative text standardization.
In the broader Indic context, INDIC-DIALECT~\cite{kumar2024indicdialect} introduced translation benchmarks for Hindi variants (Bhojpuri, Magahi, Maithili) evaluated using Weighted Finite-State Transducers (WFST)~\cite{pratap2024wfst}, while Dhasmana et al.~\cite{dhasmana2026dialect} explored cross-lingual transfer for low-resource Indic varieties.
However, research on Marathi regional dialects has remained largely restricted to speech/ASR classification~\cite{mulla2022text,pawar2026automatic}, zero-shot retrieval heuristics~\cite{gaikwad2025marathi,vyavhare2026marathi}, or preliminary rule-based text mapping~\cite{gavali2026marathi}.
Our work addresses this gap by presenting an end-to-end neural fine-tuning pipeline supported by multi-tier synthetic data augmentation and rigorous cross-dialect benchmarks.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{LLM Distillation and Multi-Tier Verification}}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly leveraged to alleviate data scarcity across generation tasks~\cite{wang2022selfinstruct,taori2023alpaca,mukherjee2023orca}.
Knowledge distillation and synthetic data expansion enable the training of compact, efficient task-specific models using outputs generated by larger teacher architectures~\cite{ho2023distilling,pecher2023better,degibert2024scaling}.
However, applying unconstrained synthetic generation to low-resource regional dialects introduces substantial risks of hallucination, non-standard orthographic noise, and language leakage~\cite{ji2023survey}.
We mitigate these risks by combining Gemma-4 generation with a morphologically tailored prompt design and a closed-loop, multi-tier verification engine to guarantee dataset integrity prior to neural fine-tuning.

\section{\textcolor{black}{Methodology}}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Target Dialects and Linguistic Properties}}

The following Marathi dialects from the RESPIN corpus were selected for this study, as they exhibit unique lexical and phonetic variations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Southern Konkan}: Shortening of final-word vowels, softening of dental consonants, and different auxiliary verb forms are its key characteristics. 
    Lexical differences include vowel shifts: standard \emph{जातो} (``he goes'') becomes \emph{जातां}; standard \emph{कुठे} (``where'') becomes \emph{कुठंय}. 
    \item \textbf{Northern Konkan}: Since it is surrounded by neighboring Gujarati and Bhili language families, it tends to exhibit significant consonant variations.
    Few examples are: standard \emph{कशाला} (``why'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{कसाले}; standard \emph{मुलगा} (``boy'') $\rightarrow$ Northern Konkan \emph{पोर}.
    \item \textbf{Varhadi}: Spoken in the regions of Vidarbha accompanied with verb suffix modifications.
    Standard questioning \emph{का?} (``why?'') becomes \emph{काऊन?}; verbal forms exhibit noticeable patterns.
    Varhadi maintains closest structural similarity to Standard Marathi as compared to other dialects since the transformations are more systematic.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Original Corpus Curation and Foundation in RESPIN}}

Our primary data source is the RESPIN-S1.0 initiative, developed by researchers at IISc Bangalore.
RESPIN provides audio recordings and their transcripts in agriculture and banking domains for different Indian languages and their dialects.
However, the corpus contains only raw dialect transcripts---it does not provide aligned dialect-to-standard Marathi translations, which are essential for training normalization models.

To bridge this gap, we used Gemma-4 (12B configuration, accessed via Ollama, run locally) to translate each dialect transcript into Standard Marathi, thereby constructing the original parallel corpus.
The unique prompts from each dialect partition (5,838 for Southern Konkan, 5,825 for Northern Konkan, and 5,330 for Varhadi) were individually processed through the LLM translation pipeline described in Section~\ref{subsec:pipeline}, yielding 16,163 verified parallel pairs across three dialects.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{LLM-Driven Translation and Verification Pipeline}}
\label{subsec:pipeline}

Both the original parallel corpus construction (Phase~1) and the subsequent synthetic expansion (Phase~2) rely on the same LLM-driven translation and verification pipeline.
In Phase~1, each RESPIN dialect transcript is translated into Standard Marathi to create the initial parallel pairs.
In Phase~2, additional synthetic dialect--standard pairs are generated from the same source transcripts to expand the training corpus.
The pipeline employs the following design aspects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1-Prompt-Per-Sentence Architecture}: Instead of batching multiple pairs per API call (which risks contextual loss), each sentence is processed separately.
    \item \textbf{Detailed Prompting Strategy}: System prompt is designed for the output to preserve exact meaning, context regarding the domain, proper nouns, and numerical values from the input with a set of 5-8 few-shot examples per dialect.
    \item \textbf{Temperature Control}: Generation temperature is set to 0.3 with top-$p$ = 0.9 for optimal results.
\end{itemize}
All translations are generated using Gemma-4 under the 12B configuration accessed via Ollama, run locally.
Every generated pair---whether from Phase~1 or Phase~2---undergoes a three-tier verification and correction pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Rule-Based Syntactic Filter.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Devanagari Script Integrity}: Both source and target must have at least 90\% Devanagari Unicode characters that lie in the \texttt{U+0900--U+097F} range, that automatically get rid of unsolicited characters.
        \item \emph{Length Ratio Constraint}: $0.5 \le |L_{\text{dialect}}| / |L_{\text{standard}}| \le 2.0$ to avoid hallucinations.
        \item \emph{Non-Identity Constraint}: $L_{\text{dialect}} \neq L_{\text{standard}}$ to flag identical string copies.
        \item \emph{Minimum Token Count}: Both source and target must contain at least 3 whitespace-separated tokens.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: LLM Semantic Verification.} A secondary round of evaluation was performed using \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant} (accessed via Groq API).
    The verification happens on a scale of 1--5 score that considers semantics, hallucinations, multilingual contamination, and complete standardization of dialects. Pairs that score $< 4.0$ are rejected or routed for further correction.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Loop Correction Engine.} Pairs that are flagged by Tier 1 or Tier 2 enter a correction loop: Step~1 generates corrected translations using specialised prompts based on the pre-defined reason of identified failure (e.g., ``Hindi leakage detected'' or ``Incomplete normalization''); Step~2 verifies the corrected sentences again, following the complete discard of pairs that fail the second audit pass.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Sequence-to-Sequence Model Conditioning and Fine-Tuning Setup}}

\textcolor{blue}{To transform pre-trained seq2seq language architectures into dedicated dialect normalizers, models are fine-tuned using PyTorch and HuggingFace's \texttt{Seq2SeqTrainer} under a 5-fold cross-validation scheme on an 85/15 stratified train-test split. To condition the models to act specifically as text normalizers rather than generic machine translators, training incorporates task-specific normalization conditioning tags and dialect-source identifiers:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Task Normalization Prefixes}: Input sequences are prepended with a task-specific normalization tag (\texttt{<normalize>}) that signals the sequence-to-sequence encoder to execute dialect-to-standard text transformation.}
    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Dialect-Source Identification Tags}: Source sentences are prefixed with dialect markers---\texttt{<D1>} for Malvani / Southern Konkan, \texttt{<D2>} for Ahirani / Northern Konkan, and \texttt{<D4>} for Varhadi. These special tokens condition the encoder's self-attention weights with dialect-specific morpho-syntactic boundaries.}
    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Target Standardization Tags}: Decoder target generation is conditioned using standard language tags (\texttt{<standard\_mr>}), enforcing strict output alignment into formal Standard Written Marathi (\emph{शुद्ध पुणेरी मराठी}).}
\end{itemize}

\textcolor{blue}{Fine-tuning is performed using AdaFactor (for mT5-small, peak learning rate $5 \times 10^{-4}$) and AdamW (for IndicBART, peak learning rate $5 \times 10^{-5}$) with linear warmup and cosine decay. Training uses label smoothing of $0.1$, dynamic padding up to maximum length 128, and beam search decoding with a beam size of 4.}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Evaluation Protocol}}

For stable performance during development, models were validated using 5-Fold Cross-Validation on an 85/15 stratified split of the train set.
The final reports were generated on the official held-out test set provided by RESPIN after training the model on the complete dataset, which had around 2,170 utterances across target dialects.
This setup is applied across all dialects on the original transcript-based data and their synthetically expanded versions.
Results are reported using metrics that are evaluated using SacreBLEU~\cite{post2018sacrebleu} and Word Error Rate (WER) scripts:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BLEU} (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy): BLEU-4 with 4-gram precision, brevity penalty, with an exponential smoothing method.
    \item \textbf{chrF++}: Character n-gram F-score combined with word unigrams and bigrams.
    \item \textbf{Normalized WER / CER}: Word Error Rate and Character Error Rate after Devanagari Unicode script standardization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Model Architecture Analysis}}

The 69.51 vs.\ 57.12 BLEU gap on the expanded multi-dialect task (+12.39 absolute) can be attributed to three key architectural differences:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vocabulary Coverage}: mT5's 250K vocabulary provides 3.9$\times$ more subword units than IndicBART's 64K, enabling finer-grained tokenization of morphologically complex Marathi dialectal forms. This is critical for dialect normalization, where subtle suffix and infix changes must be captured at the subword level.
    \item \textbf{Positional Encoding}: mT5 uses relative positional biases, which generalize better to variable-length sequences typical of dialectal text where sentence length may differ substantially between dialect and standard forms.
    \item \textbf{Conditioning Overhead}: IndicBART requires explicit language tokens (\texttt{<2mr>}) that add conditioning complexity, whereas mT5's pure text-to-text format enables direct mapping without task-specific architectural constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\textcolor{black}{Conclusion}}

\textcolor{blue}{This study introduces a scalable, reproducible framework for multi-dialect Marathi text normalization, addressing a critical research gap where Indic sub-dialects have remained severely unbenchmarked compared to established European and Arabic dialect normalization efforts. By replacing traditional, prohibitively expensive manual expert annotations with an automated synthetic pipeline, we present a comprehensive investigation of cross-dialectal variance, multi-tier verification efficacy, cross-dialect parameter interference, and pre-trained multilingual sequence-to-sequence performance on low-resource Indic varieties.}

\textcolor{blue}{Our empirical findings confirm that normalization difficulty varies substantially across regional varieties due to distinct lexical, phonetic, and morphological properties. Individual dialect performance ranged from 66.62 BLEU (Southern Konkan) to 78.73 BLEU (Varhadi) for mT5-small on the expanded multi-dialect benchmark. Crucially, multi-dialect joint training on original sparse data induced severe cross-dialect parameter interference—causing IndicBART's performance on Southern Konkan to collapse from 48.54 to 26.21 BLEU. Synthetic dataset expansion effectively eliminated this cross-dialect competition, elevating Southern Konkan performance to 52.06 BLEU for IndicBART and 66.62 BLEU for mT5-small, proving that data expansion is essential for multi-dialect model co-learning.}

\textcolor{blue}{To overcome niche domain data scarcity without relying on slow and costly human annotation, our synthetic data expansion pipeline—powered by Gemma-4 with multi-tier verification (Devanagari script filtering and LLaMA-3.1-8B semantic auditing)—successfully doubled the clean parallel corpus from 16,163 to 32,335 pairs while preserving semantic fidelity. Our ablation study demonstrates that synthetic augmentation is not merely a function of data volume; multi-tier verification flagged and discarded 4.83\% corrupted synthetic pairs. Filtering out unverified noise proved essential to prevent quality collapse, yielding a massive recovery of +14.03 BLEU (+10.95 chrF++) for IndicBART and +8.30 BLEU (+4.40 chrF++) for mT5-small compared to training on raw, unverified synthetic outputs.}

\textcolor{blue}{Models were fine-tuned using PyTorch and HuggingFace's \texttt{Seq2SeqTrainer} across a 5-fold cross-validation protocol with AdamW optimization and linear learning rate decay. To condition sequence-to-sequence architectures to operate specifically as dialect normalizers rather than generic machine translators, fine-tuning incorporates task-specific normalization conditioning tags and dialect-source identifiers (e.g., \texttt{<normalize>} task prefixes paired with dialect markers such as \texttt{<D1>}, \texttt{<D2>}, and \texttt{<D4>}). These special normalization tokens explicitly inform the encoder of the source dialect's morpho-syntactic boundaries while enforcing strict target standardization (\texttt{<standard\_mr>}) in the decoder, enabling precise sequence transduction into Standard Written Marathi.}

\textcolor{blue}{Evaluation on the held-out 2,170-utterance \texttt{IISc\_RESPIN\_test\_mr} benchmark confirms strong out-of-domain generalization across agricultural and financial text, with fine-tuned models reaching 76.50 BLEU for IndicBART and 73.48 BLEU for mT5-small. Importantly, models trained on synthetically expanded dialectal pairs maintained near-perfect normalization on Standard Written Marathi (97.39 BLEU and 1.37\% WER for mT5-small), demonstrating that audited synthetic data expansion enhances dialectal coverage without causing catastrophic forgetting of standard language orthography.}

\textcolor{blue}{Benchmarking pre-trained multilingual sequence-to-sequence architectures on the expanded benchmark establishes new state-of-the-art baselines: google/mT5-small (300M) achieved 69.51 BLEU and 84.58 chrF++ on the combined multi-dialect dataset, outperforming IndicBART (244M) by +12.39 BLEU. This performance margin is driven by key architectural advantages: mT5's 3.9$\times$ larger subword vocabulary (250K vs.\ 64K tokens) and relative positional biases accommodate agglutinative dialectal suffix merges far more effectively than IndicBART's absolute sinusoidal encodings. Ultimately, this work provides a scalable, verified blueprint for expanding and benchmarking low-resource Indic sub-dialects without reliance on manual human annotation.}

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Limitations}}

This work has several limitations that should be acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Synthetic data generation relies on LLM inference pipelines (Gemma-4 via local/hosted endpoints), introducing computational resource and latency considerations for large-scale replication.
    \item While we benchmarked two encoder-decoder architectures (mT5-small and IndicBART), decoder-only LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-3, Gemma-4) have not been evaluated for direct zero-shot or fine-tuned text normalization.
    \item Evaluation is currently focused on intrinsic text normalization metrics (BLEU, chrF++, WER); downstream task evaluations (e.g., ASR post-processing, translation) remain for future work.
    \item Synthetic augmentation exhibited a slight negative yield on Varhadi ($-$0.84 BLEU for mT5-small), suggesting that for already high-performing dialects, over-generalized suffix rules in synthetic data may slightly dilute precision---highlighting the need for targeted augmentation strategies.
\end{enumerate}
